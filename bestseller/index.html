Awaiting date
01The problem

A publishing contract is permission to be discovered. It is not discovery.

Your house will publish somewhere between eighty and three hundred titles this year. The marketing budget was allocated before your name came up in a meeting. What you were given is real and it matters. It is also the floor, not the campaign.

  • The publisher gets it into stores. Nobody gets it into conversations.
  • Your launch plan is a cover reveal, one press release and two bookstore events.
  • Amazon shows nine reviews. Goodreads shows four. All thirteen were written by people who know you.
  • You are doing the outreach yourself, and every message reads like a person selling their own book.
  • Twelve weeks in, the title moves to backlist. Backlist does not get invited anywhere.

None of this is a book problem. Every part of it is a demand problem. And demand, unlike the manuscript, has a deadline.

A book nobody is talking about is indistinguishable from a book nobody read.Readers cannot tell the difference, and they do not try.

On availability and processing fluencyPeople do not weigh the quality of a book they have not read. They weigh how often they have encountered it, and they treat that frequency as evidence.

What you lose by waiting
02What closes

Six doors. They all close in the same quarter.

None of these are decided by how good the book is. Every one of them is decided by whether there is visible demand around it at the moment somebody is choosing. That moment is not scheduled around your convenience.

01

The keynote

Speaking fees are paid for an audience, not for a book. Corporates and conferences book people whose ideas are already circulating. A published author with no visible following is priced as a guest, not a keynote.

Who is repeating your idea when you are not in the room?
02

The second book

Your next advance is priced off this launch. Sell-through, review volume, media coverage and follower growth are the numbers an acquisitions meeting actually looks at. A quiet first book makes the second one harder to sell than the first.

What will your first book have proved?
03

The festival stage

Jaipur, Kerala, Bangalore, Times, Kolkata. Programmers build session lists months ahead from writers who are visible now, and they are looking for a session, not a book. No angle, no invitation.

What panel are you obviously the answer to?
04

The adaptation

Production houses and streaming commissioners option books that arrive with an audience attached. The pitch is never the plot alone. It is the plot plus proof that people already care about this story.

Does anyone outside publishing know this book exists?
05

The authority

This is the expensive one. Without visible demand, a real imprint reads exactly like a vanity press. Readers, event bookers and journalists cannot see your contract. They can only see your ratings, your coverage and your page.

What does your name look like to a stranger searching it?
06

The reorder

Physical retail restocks on early sell-through, and online ranking compounds on early velocity. Both are decided in weeks. No pull in the first weeks means no reorder, no second print, and a title that quietly disappears from the shelf.

What happens in week three?

Every one of these doors is opened by the same thing: enough of the right people encountering you and your book, often enough, in the weeks that matter. That is a build. It is not luck and it is not a post.

This module builds authentic review volume through the readers you actually have, and it manages the page as an asset rather than leaving it to chance. Nothing bought, nothing faked, because a detected review scheme costs more than it ever returns.

  • Handling of critical reviews without damaging the author

09Is this you

This is built for a narrow set of authors.

  • Your manuscript is approved and publication is within the next two months.
  • Your book came out in the last three months and it is quieter than you expected.
  • You are published by a recognised house and it still feels like nothing happened.
  • You want speaking, teaching or advisory work to follow the book.
  • You intend to write a second book and want the first one to make that easier.
  • You are uncomfortable promoting yourself and you know it is costing you.
  • Your Amazon and Goodreads pages look thinner than the book deserves.
  • You have a real idea in the book, not just a story, and it belongs in wider conversation.

If three or more of these are true, the window is already working against you, and the cost of the delay is larger than the cost of the work.

Not built for

  • Self-published or vanity-press titles with no retail distribution.
  • Authors who want followers rather than readers.
  • Anyone looking for guaranteed rankings, bought reviews or bot-driven numbers.
  • Manuscripts still looking for a publisher. Come back when you have the contract.
  • Books published more than a year ago with no new edition or hook to work with.

I say no to more enquiries than I accept, and I say it in the first conversation rather than after an invoice. If your book is outside the window or outside what this work can honestly move, I will tell you that and point you somewhere more useful.

10Before you write to me

The questions authors actually ask.

Can you guarantee my book becomes a bestseller?+

No, and you should be wary of anyone who does. Bestseller is not a certified status in India, and the lists that exist are compiled differently, weekly, and often from partial data. What I can commit to is the work: the outreach volume, the seeding, the coverage pipeline, the content, the podcast bookings, and the platform you keep afterwards.

What I have found is that the authors who get called bestsellers are almost never the ones with the best books. They are the ones whose books were impossible to miss during the eight weeks it mattered.

My publisher already has a marketing team. Is this not duplication?+

Your publisher markets a list. I build one author. Those are different jobs with different success measures. Their publicist is responsible for perhaps forty titles this season and will do good work on the announcement, the listings and the trade. Almost none of them are resourced to build your positioning, your website, your podcast circuit or your review base.

I work alongside your publicist, share what I am doing, and never approach a contact they have already worked without telling them. Most publicists are relieved. They get an author who arrives with assets and angles instead of one asking what the plan is.

My book is already out. Is it too late?+

Within three months of publication, no. The compressed version runs positioning, seeding and content in parallel from week one, and the press angles shift from launch coverage to the argument in the book, which many desks prefer anyway. Reviews, podcasts, festivals and adaptation interest are all still fully available.

Past six months it gets materially harder, and I will say so honestly rather than take the engagement. Past a year, the honest answer is usually to build the platform for the next book instead.

How much of my time does this take?+

Two working sessions of roughly two hours each at the start, one long interview I record and mine for content, and then two to three hours a week for approvals, interviews and podcast recordings. The point of the engagement is that a team does the work, and you do the parts only you can do, which is being interesting in conversation.

Do you write the posts, or do I?+

My team drafts, you approve. Everything is built from your own material: the manuscript, your interviews, the recorded session, your prior writing. The test I hold it to is whether someone who knows you would believe you wrote it. If it does not sound like you, it does not go out, because a voice that reads as ghostwritten costs more credibility than it buys reach.

Do you buy reviews or run engagement pods?+

No. Purchased reviews violate Amazon and Goodreads policy, get detected, and the removal takes your legitimate reviews with it. The reputational cost to a debut author is permanent and public. Everything here is earned: real copies with real readers, and a request sequence that converts the readers you already have.

Why six authors a quarter?+

Because the same journalists, hosts, festival programmers and book clubs are being approached for every author I represent. Beyond a small number, I would be competing with myself for the same attention and diluting what I can do for each person. It is a capacity limit, not a scarcity tactic.
