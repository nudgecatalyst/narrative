Movement · A Nudge Catalyst notebook

Mechanical watches, for people who are not watch people.

Eight things a month about the machines on people’s wrists and the houses that have been arguing about them for two hundred years.

No specifications. No investment advice. No test to pass before you are allowed to find it interesting. If a sentence needs a glossary, it gets rewritten.

Eight things, once a month. No forwarding your address anywhere, no second list, one click to leave.

02Edition one · September 2026
01 The insight

Being admired and being known are two different currencies.

I scored a hundred watches on ten separate measures. Two of them were how much serious watchmakers respect the thing, and how many people in an ordinary room would recognise the name. I expected those two numbers to move together. Better watches, better known. That is how we assume the world works.

They move together almost not at all. Across all hundred, the relationship between the two is 0.17, which in plain terms means knowing one tells you nearly nothing about the other.

Cartier scores 9 out of 10 on public recognition and around 6 with the people who actually make watches. Grand Seiko, a Japanese house whose finishing is the quiet envy of Switzerland, scores 10 on craft and 4 on recognition. Neither is failing. They are simply spending in different currencies, and neither currency buys the other.

The same idea, outside watches

Awareness and esteem are not the same asset. They are not built the same way, they do not decay at the same rate, and buying one does not get you the other.

  • Awareness is bought with reach. Esteem is earned with proof, and only from people who can evaluate the proof.
  • A company can be famous and unrespected, which is fragile. It can be respected and invisible, which is expensive.
  • Most brand budgets are spent as though one converts into the other. It rarely does.
Ask this about your own business Which of the two are you actually short of, and are you spending against the one you are short of, or the one that is easier to measure?
02 The trivia

Rolex quietly supplies engines to its own competitors.

The engine inside a mechanical watch is called the movement, and making one well is the hardest part of the business. Most brands buy theirs. A very small number make their own. Almost nobody knows who is really making whose.

There is a company called Kenissi. It was created by Tudor, which is Rolex’s younger sibling. Rolex holds a stake in it. Chanel, the fashion house, owns twenty per cent of it. And Kenissi makes movements for Tudor, Breitling, Chanel, TAG Heuer, Norqain and Fortis.

Read that again slowly. The most secretive company in watchmaking is, through a sister organisation, the engine supplier to several of the brands sitting opposite it in every shop window in the world.

Three more that reorder the map

  • Zenith made the movement inside the Rolex Daytona from 1988 to 2000. The most wanted watch on earth ran on a rival’s engine for twelve years.
  • The Omega that went to the Moon used a movement made by a firm called Lemania, which also supplied Patek Philippe and Vacheron Constantin. Lemania now belongs to Breguet.
  • Jaeger-LeCoultre spent much of the twentieth century supplying Patek Philippe, Vacheron Constantin, Audemars Piguet and Cartier. The industry’s most exclusive names shared a workshop.
Why it matters Almost every industry has a supply map that contradicts its marketing map. Finding it is usually the fastest way to understand who actually holds the power.
03 The tool · The Gap

One number that tells you whether you are buying the thing, or the signal.

Score anything twice. Once on what it actually is: how well it is made, how hard it was to make, whether the design mattered. Once on what it says about you: how many people recognise it, what it costs, how hard it is to get.

Subtract the first from the second. That is the Gap. A large positive number means you are paying mostly for the signal. A large negative number means you are getting far more object than anyone around you will notice.

Neither is wrong. Wanting to be seen is a legitimate reason to buy something. But you should know which one you are doing.

It works on almost anything

  • A car, a handbag, a school, a job title, a conference, a consultant.
  • Positive Gap: a business school with a famous name and ordinary teaching.
  • Negative Gap: the supplier who does the best work in the category and cannot get a meeting.
  • The interesting money is almost always sitting in the negative column, and the interesting risk in the positive one.
Sort the same seventeen watches by

CraftHow well it is made, how much of the engine the house designed itself, whether the design changed the category, and what watchmakers think of it.

StatusHow many people recognise the name, what it resells for, how hard it is to get, and what it costs.

04 The campaign

Chopard does not sponsor the Cannes Film Festival. It makes the trophy.

Since 1998 the Palme d’Or, the award every serious film-maker on earth wants, has been made by hand in a Swiss watch workshop. Nineteen carats of ethical gold, a stem shaped like a palm frond, set on a piece of rock crystal cut to look like a cushion.

Consider what that buys. Every year, the most photographed moment in world cinema is a person holding a Chopard. Not a Chopard advertisement near the moment. The moment itself.

A sponsor can always be outbid. A logo on a backdrop belongs to whoever pays most next year. But nobody can outbid the maker of the trophy, because the trophy is not media space. It is the object the whole event exists to hand over.

The pattern underneath

Do not rent attention next to the thing people care about. Become a part of the thing people care about.

  • Omega did the same by becoming the watch NASA chose after anonymous testing, rather than the watch that advertised near astronauts.
  • Van Cleef & Arpels built an actual school of jewellery arts with four campuses. A competitor can outspend a campaign. It cannot outspend an institution that already has alumni.
  • These moves are slow, expensive and permanent, which is precisely why they cannot be copied by anyone in a hurry.
The question it asks of you In your category, what is the object everyone is trying to be photographed with, and could you be the one who makes it rather than the one who buys space beside it?
05 The story

A man of sixty-six restarted his family’s firm after forty-two years of it being dead.

A. Lange & Söhne was founded in 1845 in Glashütte, a small town in Saxony. For a century it made watches good enough that German collectors put them beside anything Switzerland produced. Then the Second World War flattened the workshops, and in 1948 the Soviet administration expropriated the company and folded it into a state combine. The name disappeared.

Walter Lange, the founder’s great-grandson, fled to the West. He spent his working life doing other things. And in 1990, months after the Berlin Wall came down, he went back to a town he had not been able to enter for four decades and refounded a company that had not existed since before he was thirty. He was sixty-six years old.

The first four watches were shown in 1994. Today the house is regarded, by the people who make watches for a living, as roughly the finest in the world. The whole thing rests on one man deciding, at an age when most people are finishing, that forty-two years of absence did not count as an ending.

What it teaches, quietly

  • Heritage is not a story a brand tells. It is an asset a brand either kept or lost, and Lange lost everything except the belief.
  • Houses that dismantled their archives during the hard years spent the next thirty buying their own old watches back at auction.
  • What survived the forty-two years was not machinery or drawings. It was a set of principles specific enough to be rebuilt from.
Worth sitting with If your organisation vanished for forty years, what exactly would someone need in order to start it again, and does that thing exist anywhere in writing?
06 The object

The Grand Seiko White Birch. A nine thousand dollar watch that out-crafts things costing ten times more.

The face of this watch is textured to look like a stand of birch trees outside the workshop in northern Japan where it is made. Not printed. Stamped from a die cut so precisely that the pattern catches light the way bark does, differently at every angle, so the watch is never quite the same colour twice in a day.

The hands and the hour markers are cut and polished by hand until their edges are mirrors. Hold it near a window and the markers flash black, because a surface polished that flat reflects nothing back at you. Watchmakers call this distortion-free polishing and it is done with a spinning tin plate, by people who have spent years learning it.

Inside, the balance wheel oscillates ten times a second rather than the usual eight, which is why the seconds hand appears to glide rather than step. On my hundred-watch model it scores 9.1 out of 10 for pure craft, above almost everything from Geneva. It ranks 57th overall, dragged down entirely by the fact that most people have never heard of it.

House
Grand Seiko, Shizukuishi, Japan. A division of Seiko, elevated to its own brand in 2017.
Reference
SLGH005, known everywhere as White Birch.
Price
Around $9,000 to $10,000. Roughly ₹8 to ₹9 lakh.
Craft score
9.1 / 10. Higher than the Rolex Daytona, the Cartier Crash and most of the top twenty.
Status score
4.5 / 10. One of the largest gaps between merit and recognition in the entire category.
The catch
Nobody at the table will know what it is. Whether that is the flaw or the entire appeal is a question about you, not about the watch.
Why this one, this month It is the clearest object lesson in the insight above. Grand Seiko’s problem was never the product. It was thirty years of selling extraordinary watches through channels that told customers they were ordinary ones.
07 The word

“In-house.” The industry’s favourite boast, and mostly a smaller deal than it sounds.

What it means. That the brand designed and built the movement itself rather than buying one. It is the single most repeated claim in watch marketing, and it is usually said in the tone of a trump card.

Why it exists. For most of the twentieth century almost everyone bought movements from the same handful of Swiss suppliers. When the largest of those began cutting off outside customers in 2010, brands were forced to build their own capability, and then to charge for having done so.

What the numbers say. Across my hundred watches, ones with in-house movements average 7.44 overall. Hybrids, meaning a bought base that the brand has substantially reworked, average 7.26. That difference is noise. Watches on plain bought movements do score materially lower, at 5.70, so the claim is not empty. It is just far smaller than the price premium attached to it.

Two things to notice

  • The Cartier Tank Louis runs a bought movement and is one of the most desired objects in the category. Nobody has ever cared.
  • A four and a half thousand dollar Louis Erard, on a bought Sellita base, scores 8 out of 10 with serious collectors. It got there by collaborating with a watchmaker who had enormous reputation and no distribution, while Louis Erard had distribution and no reputation. Each rented what the other could not build.
The transferable version Every industry has a vertical-integration boast. It is usually real, usually worth something, and almost never worth what the price list claims. Ask what the customer can actually perceive. If they cannot perceive it, it is a cost, not a differentiator.
08 The question

What is on your wrist that you would never sell, and what does it actually have to do with time?

Almost nobody’s answer is about the watch. It is about a father, a first salary, a promotion nobody else noticed, a person who is no longer here. Send me yours. The best ones run in the next edition, with your name or without it, exactly as you prefer.

03Share your watch story

It does not have to be an expensive watch. It has to be a true one.

A Casio from 1998 counts. A watch you inherited and have never worn counts. A watch you sold and regret counts most of all. Three or four sentences is plenty. I read every one of these myself and I reply.

Write it the way you would tell it out loud. I would rather have it rough and true than polished.

07Who writes it, and why

I am not a watch expert. That is rather the point.

I am Shubho Broto Das. I have spent twenty years building brands, most recently as head of brand and communication for a deep-tech defence company in India, and before that at McKinsey, Essex Lake Group, Ennovent and Xynteo. My work is making complicated things understood by people who have every reason to ignore them.

Mechanical watches are the purest brand-equity business that exists. The metal in one rarely costs a fraction of the price. Everything above that is story, craft and belief, which means the industry is a laboratory for everything I do for a living. I started reading it as research. I kept reading it because it is genuinely wonderful, and because almost every piece of writing about it is designed to make you feel excluded.

Movement is the notebook. It is free, it will stay free, and it is not selling you a watch.
