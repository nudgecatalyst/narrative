In one paragraphWhat is an Authentic Relating Games workshop, and who is it for?

Bond is a half-day, in-person Authentic Relating Games workshop facilitated by Shubho Broto Das of Nudge Catalyst, based in Bengaluru and run at your own venue anywhere in India. Twelve to eighteen senior people play a sequence of structured conversational games that build the thing their calendars never make room for: honest, unguarded contact with each other. It is built for boards and CXO leadership teams, investor portfolio founders who meet quarterly and still do not know each other, and member forums and peer networks where trust is assumed rather than built.

Not therapy. Not improv. Not an adventure activity. No role-play, no touch, no forced disclosure, and an explicit pass on every game. It is designed for guarded, accomplished people over forty, and it works because it never asks them to open up. It asks them to play. You pick the date, the city and the venue. A corporate team bonding workshop that leaves a shared vocabulary behind rather than a shared photograph.

01The room you already sit in

You could recite their burn rate from memory. You could not name one thing that frightens them.

Senior rooms in India are superb at information and terrible at contact. Everyone is briefed. Nobody is known.

It is not a character flaw. It is what twenty years of being competent does to a person. You learn early that the version of you that gets promoted is edited, you get very good at the edit, and by forty-five the edit runs by itself.

The cost lands somewhere you do not connect to it. The co-founder conversation you keep postponing. The leadership team that agrees in the room and disagrees in the car park. The forum where everyone is doing well, always, uniformly well.

Then someone says the team needs to bond, and books a resort.

The offsite that ends with a bonfire and a group photograph.Everybody had a nice time. On Monday the same three things go unsaid.
The forum check-in that is a status update in disguise.Nine people report they are broadly on track. At least one is lying, and it is usually more than one.
The leadership team that has never disagreed in front of you.Alignment and silence look identical from the head of the table.

02What Authentic Relating actually is

Games with a rule, a timer and an exit. That is the whole trick.

The rulecarries the risk, so the person does not have to.
The timerdecides when it stops, so nobody manages the exit.
The exitis stated aloud, so staying is a choice and not a trap.

Ask a fifty-two-year-old promoter to open up and you get a well-constructed nothing. Give him an instruction, a two-minute timer, a partner with the same instruction and an explicit permission to pass, and something else happens.

The rule carries the risk. He is not exposing himself, he is following an instruction like everyone else in the room. That is the doorway. Then the games get slightly harder, at the pace the room sets, and by hour three the instruction has fallen away and people are simply talking.

Nothing mystical. No talking stick, no chanting, no shoes off. The nervous system does what it does in every room: it reads whether it is safe and opens accordingly. Facilitation exists to make the room read safe faster than it otherwise would.

04The half day · hour by hour

Four hours, built as a ramp. Nobody is dropped in the deep end and nobody is left in it.

The commonest failure in a session like this is starting too deep. The room closes in the first twenty minutes and never reopens. So the arc is engineered: very low stakes for the first hour, and the room sets the ceiling after that.

0:00

Arrival and agreements

The twenty minutes that decide the other three hours and forty

Chairs in a circle, no table. What will happen, what will never happen, how to pass. Confidentiality agreed by the room rather than announced by me. Nobody introduces themselves by title, which is usually the first jolt.

0:25

Calibration

Games that ask you to notice, not to confess

Short, very low stakes, pairs rotating fast. You describe what you observe, not what you feel. Half the room relaxes inside ten minutes. The other half is still waiting for the catch, which is accounted for.

1:00

Pairs

The first real conversations, with a timer holding the frame

Four to six minutes each way, partners rotating. One speaks to a prompt, one practises a specific way of listening. The timer does the work: nobody decides when to stop, so nobody manages the exit.

1:45

The break

Fifteen minutes, and it is part of the design

Not a coffee break, a pressure valve, and where a good deal of the real conversation starts. I do not interrupt it. If it runs to twenty-five minutes, the afternoon adjusts.

2:00

Small groups

Three or four people, and the stakes rise by choice

Longer rounds, harder prompts, and the appreciation practice. Most rooms have their first genuinely unguarded moment here, in a group of four rather than a group of eighteen. Four is survivable.

3:00

The circle

Everyone at once, for the only time in the afternoon

One round, one prompt, short contributions. No cross-talk, no fixing, no advice. For most senior groups this is the first time in years fifteen people have listened without preparing a response.

3:35

Integration and re-entry

Nobody walks out mid-descent

A deliberate climb back to professional altitude. What you are taking, what you want from each other next. A one-page summary of the practices used follows afterwards. Then it ends, on time, at four hours.

05The games · what people actually do

Eight practices, ordered by stake. The room never goes further than it chooses to.

Nothing here requires touch, commercial disclosure, or talking about your childhood. Open one and see.

This is the standard arc, not a fixed one. A board that has just been through a difficult exit needs a different shape from founders meeting for the first time, and I ask enough beforehand to know which room I am walking into.

01NoticingNegligible stake+

You sit opposite someone and describe what you observe. Not what you conclude. “I notice you are sitting very straight.” Absurdly simple, and the foundation of everything after it, because most senior people skip from observation to judgement so fast that the two have fused.

  • The rule: observation only. If it contains a “because”, it is not one.
  • Builds: the gap between what happened and what you decided it meant.
  • Why first: nothing personal is revealed, so nothing is risked to begin.
Pairs4 minutesNobody has ever passed on this one.
02Bonding without wordsLow stake+

A short non-verbal round. Two people, seated, a simple structure of attention. No touch, no mime, no theatre. The one people are most sceptical about beforehand and most surprised by afterwards, because removing language removes what senior people defend themselves with best.

  • The rule: no words, no touch, and you may look away whenever you like.
  • Builds: the felt sense that the other person is a person, which language often obstructs.
  • Typical reaction: laughter in the first thirty seconds, silence in the last thirty.
Pairs2 to 3 minutesFully optional. Sitting it out is visible to nobody but me.
03What is true for me right nowLow to moderate+

Two minutes on what is going on for you at this moment. Not this quarter. This chair. It sounds easy and is the first real hurdle of the day: most people start with a summary of their week, catch themselves, and then say something true.

  • The rule: present tense. No stories about last year.
  • The listener: no response, no advice, no polite nodding. The harder job.
  • Builds: saying something unfinished out loud without managing the other person's reaction.
Pairs2 min each wayPass by saying “pass”. No explanation asked or given.
04The question you have never askedModerate+

You ask someone something you have genuinely wanted to ask and never have. They may answer it, answer part of it, or decline it, and all three are said out loud. In a leadership team this is usually where two years of politeness ends.

  • The rule: real curiosity only. A question with an accusation inside it is not one.
  • The answer: “I would rather not” is complete and respected.
  • Builds: asking directly instead of inferring for another eighteen months.
Small groups8 to 10 minutesClosely facilitated. This is where I intervene most.
05Appreciation, receivedHarder than it sounds+

Someone tells you specifically what they value about you. Your only job is to receive it. Not deflect, not return it, not make a joke, not say “arre, no no”. Reliably the hardest exercise of the afternoon for Indian senior professionals, and the one most often named at the end.

  • The rule: specific, not general. “You are great” is banned. “The way you handled March” is the form.
  • The receiver: say thank you. Nothing else. Deflection is the failure mode.
  • Builds: being seen without immediately neutralising it.
Small groups10 minutesWatch the room go quiet. It always does.
06The unsaidHigh · conditional+

A bounded practice for saying the thing you have been carrying about someone in the room. Bounded is the operative word: a specific form, a time limit, a required response. I do not run it with every group and I say beforehand whether I expect to.

  • The rule: one sentence of fact, one of impact. No interpretation, no demand.
  • The listener: receive it, reflect it back, stop. No defence in the room.
  • The safeguard: opt-in, and the room decides collectively whether it runs at all.
Small groups12 to 15 minutesSkipped without comment if the room is not there.
07If you really knew meDepth chosen by the speaker+

One round of the full circle. Each person completes the sentence once, at whatever depth they choose. The range in a single circle is enormous: one person says something about their commute, the next says something they have not told their spouse. Both are acceptable, and the room learns from the gap.

  • The rule: one contribution each. No responses, no returning to an earlier speaker.
  • The room: nothing said here is referenced afterwards unless the speaker raises it.
  • Builds: being heard by everybody at once and nothing bad happening.
Full circle15 to 20 minutesPass rate is typically zero by this point.
08The closing roundBack down to low, on purpose+

What you are taking, what you want more of from this group, one thing you will do differently. Structural rather than sentimental: a group that has spent an hour at depth needs walking back up before it goes to dinner. Ending at the bottom of the arc is how people regret a good afternoon.

  • The rule: one sentence each, forward-facing. No summing up of the day.
  • Builds: a clean exit, and commitments made to each other rather than to me.
  • Afterwards: one page of the practices this room actually used.
Full circle10 minutesEnds on time. Always.
06Designed for people who do not do this

Most connection work is built for people who already want to connect. This is built for the man with his arms folded.

Somewhere between forty and fifty, competent people acquire a specific problem. They are excellent at being useful and out of practice at being known.

It is not resistance and it is not ego, although it looks like both from outside. It is disuse. Two decades in rooms where the correct move was composure, until composure became the only available move. Hand that person a circle of chairs and the honest internal response is not “I refuse”. It is I am not sure I would know how.

The half day is engineered around that one insight. It never asks for willingness it has not built, and the safety is structural rather than emotional, which matters for a cohort that does not respond to being told a space is safe and does respond to being shown that it is bounded.

There is a quiet dividend too. When the most defended person in the room is the one who eventually says the true thing, and nothing bad happens to him, everyone else recalculates at once.

Safeguard 01

An explicit pass

Stated aloud in the first ten minutes. Anyone can pass on any question, sit out any round, and stay in the room while doing it.

Safeguard 02

Nothing leaves the room

No notes, no recording, no photographs during practice, no debrief to HR, the board or the fund. Including to whoever booked it.

Safeguard 03

Nothing commercial is asked

No numbers, no valuations, no holdings, no succession plans. Business detail is not the material.

Safeguard 04

No touch, no theatre

No trust falls, blindfolds, hugging, role-play or chanting. Seated, conversational and recognisably adult throughout.

Safeguard 05

The boss question, handled

Where a reporting line is in the room it is named in the agreements. Seniors speak later in rounds, so the room does not calibrate to the most powerful person present.

Safeguard 06

A designed exit

The last twenty-five minutes bring the group back to professional altitude. Nobody goes to dinner exposed.

Safety is not a feeling you talk a room into. It is a structure you build under it. The principle behind the whole design
07What changes · in business terms

Nobody needs an afternoon of feelings. They need Tuesday's meeting to go differently.

A half day does not rebuild a broken culture, and I will not pretend otherwise. What it reliably changes is what a specific group believes is sayable to each other, and almost every expensive organisational problem sits downstream of that.

Decisions get made twice: once in the room, once in the corridor.Objections arrive while the decision is still open, which is the only time they are worth anything.
Bad news travels upward slowly and arrives fully formed.The gap between someone knowing and someone saying shortens. At senior level that is the whole of risk management.
Portfolio founders treat each other as a directory.They treat each other as a resource, and you stop being the single point of contact.
New members take two years to belong.They belong by the next event, and renewal stops being a question.
Two senior people have an unresolved thing everyone can feel.It gets named once, in a form both can hear, with a facilitator present rather than in a performance review.
"Psychological safety" is a phrase in the values deck.The group has an experience of it and a vocabulary for asking for it.

What I will not claim: that this fixes attrition, substitutes for leadership, or survives a leader who behaves differently on Monday. The half day opens a door. Whoever runs the room decides whether it stays open.

10The questions everybody asks

Before you put this in front of anyone else.

Twelve questions in roughly the order I get them. Answered plainly, because you will have to repeat these answers to somebody who was not on the call.

What exactly is an Authentic Relating Games workshop?+

Four facilitated hours of structured conversational games, played in pairs, small groups and one full circle. Each game has an instruction, a timer and a purpose. One teaches noticing. One teaches saying the thing you normally swallow. One teaches taking a compliment without deflecting it. Nothing is performed and nobody stands on a chair.

Is this therapy?+

No. No diagnosis, no treatment, no history taking, no clinical frame. It is skills practice, closer to a language lab than a clinic. If something in the room belongs with a therapist, I say so privately and stop there.

My team is guarded and mostly over forty. Will this work?+

That is who it is built for. The first hour asks you to notice, not to confess. Nobody is asked about childhood and nobody is asked to cry. The most defended person in the room is usually the one who stays back at the end.

How long is it and where does it happen?+

Four hours, in person, at your location: offsite venue, board room, farmhouse, hotel or campus. I am based in Bengaluru and travel across India. There is no fixed date and no fixed venue, because both are yours.

What is the ideal group size?+

Twelve to eighteen. Eight works. Twenty-four works with a co-facilitator. Past thirty the circle stops being a circle and the depth drops, so I would rather run it twice.

Do you travel outside Bengaluru?+

Yes. Mumbai, Delhi, Gurugram, Hyderabad, Pune, Chennai, Goa and offsite destinations. Travel and stay on actuals, agreed before anything is confirmed.

What if someone shares something they later regret?+

Nothing leaves the room. No notes, no recording, no report to HR or to the fund. Every game has an explicit pass. And the last half hour deliberately brings the group back to professional altitude, so nobody walks out mid-descent.

Is anyone forced to participate?+

No. The pass is stated aloud at the start and it is real. Anyone can sit out any round and stay in the room. It is rarely used after the first hour, and it has to exist for the first hour to work.

We already do adventure offsites. How is this different?+

Those build a shared memory. This builds a shared vocabulary. After rafting your team has a story about the river. After this they have a way of saying the difficult thing to each other on a Tuesday.

What does it cost?+

One facilitation fee for the half day, scoped on group size and city, with travel on actuals. You get the number in the first conversation, before you have to sell it to anyone internally.

How do I pitch this internally?+

You do not have to write it. Four pitches sit on this page by room type, covering the case you make upward and the case you make to the people who will be in the room. Take the language and send it.

What happens afterwards?+

A one-page summary of the practices your group actually used, so the vocabulary survives the car park. Some groups repeat it next quarter. Some run three sessions across a quarter. None of that is committed on day one.
